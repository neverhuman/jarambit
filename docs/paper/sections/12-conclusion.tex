\section{Conclusion: From Unexpected Geometry to Valuable Products}
The future of computational engineering is larger than automated dimension tuning. It is the ability to formulate and explore geometric families, discover useful alternatives, explain their tradeoffs, and connect every important conclusion to evidence. Jarambit illustrates both the ambition and the discipline this future requires. Its engineering depth becomes legible when 16 diagnostic predicates, ten overlapping requirement groups, 18 heterogeneous evaluator columns and 178 assurance definitions are explained through their actual roles rather than advertised as one large test count.

The retained evidence supports a substantive but bounded conclusion. The highest historical search rank failed later strict assessment. A separately retained assessed example reports close matched solid-solver agreement while neither plate GCI estimate is admitted. Together, these cases show why ranking, implementation agreement and discretization evidence need separate interpretations. The historical battery, weighted summaries and full-birth population can be reconstructed within their declared scopes; they do not establish a qualified product, global optimality, agent-discovered superior representations, unprecedented manufacturing tolerance or physical reliability.

The expansive opportunity lies in joining representation research with manufacturing and measurement. Agent assistance could help engineers describe richer design grammars, expose hidden dependencies and inspect alternatives whose complexity exceeds practical manual enumeration. Evolutionary search could then explore those grammars while independent models and experiments determine which observations deserve confidence. Metal additive manufacturing, including titanium processes, could make some of the resulting forms practical to investigate. None of those steps removes the need to characterize what was actually produced.

The possible value is considerable. Aerospace and spaceflight fixtures could benefit from carefully characterized mass and stiffness tradeoffs. Robotics could benefit from integrated structures whose interfaces and compliance are designed together. Thermal-management research could investigate complex flow and conduction geometries. Medical-device research could explore patient-specific forms while meeting demanding biocompatibility, inspection and regulatory obligations. Scientific instruments and energy systems could use geometries that integrate functions previously spread across multiple parts. These are prospective civilian opportunities, each requiring its own model domain, measurements and qualification.

The strongest vision is therefore not that a machine will produce an object no human could ever imagine. It is that engineers will be able to investigate more useful possibilities than they could enumerate by hand, understand why selected possibilities appear promising, and measure whether those promises survive contact with manufacturing and the physical world. The path to tighter tolerances and higher-value products runs through metrology, uncertainty, independent scrutiny and reproducible evidence as well as through intelligence and compute.

That combination can support a more ambitious engineering practice: broader exploration, more explicit reasoning, faster rejection of unsupported claims, and a clearer route from a surprising digital form to a product whose value can be demonstrated. The next milestone is an independently reproduced benign research result, followed by appropriately scoped physical evidence. The ambition is expansive; the evidence must remain exact.
