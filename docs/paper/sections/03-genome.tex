\section{Representation and Candidate Identity}
\label{sec:genome}

\subsection{A Gene Is a Semantic Contract}
A gene requires an explicit coordinate index, physical or discrete type, unit, domain, coupling law, and decoder revision. A serialized floating-point token alone cannot distinguish a millimeter displacement from an angle, a feature-count selector, or an architecture switch. Those semantics determine what a stored genome means when it is decoded.

The documented native schema stores 128 numeric coordinates and one categorical material field. Some numeric coordinates express continuous parameters; others are interpreted through thresholds or discretization. The nominal coordinate count is therefore neither the number of independently active parameters nor a measure of geometric freedom. Field roles are summarized below; exact domains and decoding rules require the producer revision attached to each cohort. Historical-producer details are retained in Supplement~\ref{supp:representation-search}, without attributing them to every executed workflow.

\subsection{Coupling and Conditional Activity}
Clamping, thresholding, and coupled bounds distinguish stored coordinates from the geometry they produce. In the bound historical producer, the continuous-rail switch is thresholded, the active knot count is floored and bounded, and enabling the layer disables several legacy features. When it is disabled, knot coordinates are set to stock thickness during clamping. The decoder and its configuration thus belong to the interpretation of a record, rather than being incidental implementation details. A numeric change can be inactive, discretized away, or constrained by another coordinate; changed-coordinate counts alone do not establish geometric change.

\subsection{Birth, Genome, Membership, and Export}
A birth identifies a recorded generation event; a genome serialization identifies stored coordinate and material values; a membership identifies a population-slot occurrence; and an assessed export identifies its own geometry and assessment bundle. These objects can share an individual identifier without sharing a denominator. The historical cohort, for example, retains repeated genome serializations and repeated appearances of earlier births. A display mesh, a plate mesh, and a solid submodel likewise represent distinct discretizations, each requiring its own source binding (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}).

The inheritance ledger counts changed numeric coordinates by exact numeric inequality on parsed CSV values. Its raw-coordinate $L_2$ norms mix units and discrete semantics. They are audit summaries of stored coordinates, not physical distances between shapes; the detailed edge ledger is retained in Supplement~\ref{supp:representation-search}.
