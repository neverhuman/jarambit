\section{Introduction: Intelligence in the Design Loop}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{n} engineer usually begins with an intelligible shape, changes a manageable set of dimensions, and evaluates the consequences. Computational design permits a different order: specify a representation, define observations and preferences, and explore many admissible variations before selecting a shape for closer study. The intellectual shift is substantial. A designer can reason about a family of possible objects rather than committing early to a single object. Yet the family, the evaluator, and the definition of improvement remain human engineering choices.

We use \emph{intelligent design} to mean this computational engineering process: representations, search, numerical models, software agents, and human judgment working together. The phrase carries no claim about biological origins, autonomous scientific authority, or an algorithm's understanding of physical reality. Intelligence is expressed through how the process proposes, checks, records, and revises designs. A compelling surface alone cannot establish that the process has succeeded.

Jarambit provides a useful case study because it contains more engineering machinery than its front-page story readily conveys. Its current crescent representation has 128 numerical genes and a material field. A decoded candidate is evaluated through plate and local-solid models, geometric diagnostics, requirement comparisons, software controls, and retained evidence records. A browser studio presents CAD and scorecards. Different search lanes coexist, and historical scoring conventions remain visible in the repository \cite{repo}. The depth is real, but the categories require careful explanation.

Three questions organize this paper. First, how does a numerical gene become an identifiable CAD artifact with interpretable observations? Second, what do the many named engineering checks actually establish, individually and collectively? Third, what evidence would be needed to support stronger claims about agent-assisted representation design, continuous optimization, manufacturing precision, and practical value? These questions separate a system's architecture from the empirical claims that can currently be defended.

The distinction is consequential. A software control may have executed successfully without evaluating a particular CAD. A local-solid result may be complete while its numerical finding is unfavorable. A threshold comparison may reuse the same observation as several other comparisons. An archive can preserve its best recorded score while its new population becomes worse. A mathematically favorable design can still be physically unvalidated. All of these cases appear naturally in computational engineering; none should be hidden by a large total test count.

\subsection{Contributions and Evidence Boundary}
The first contribution is an end-to-end explanation of the existing representation, search, assessment, and reporting lifecycle. The second is a complete taxonomy of the CAD diagnostics and battery evaluators, including their overlaps and interpretation limits. The third is a reproducible retrospective reconciliation of selected retained data: a fixed historical battery, one weighted cohort, and a separately identified population dataset. The fourth is a research agenda for agent-assisted genome design that preserves the independence of proposal and evaluation.

These contributions do not include a new optimizer, a new physical objective, an improved weapon, a manufacturing process, or a newly qualified product. The repository's original application is a titanium utility-knife research study. This paper documents its computational assessment architecture without prescribing geometry, physical loading, or performance improvements. Broader applications discussed later are prospective civilian research opportunities, not demonstrated transfers of qualification.

\subsection{Why This Is a Research Problem}
As search becomes cheaper, the number of proposed designs can grow much faster than the amount of trustworthy evidence about each one. When representations become more expressive, an evaluator may be applied outside the domain for which its assumptions were reasonable. When agents generate changes to code or schemas, a search can alter the meaning of the very variables being optimized. These are research problems in experiment design, numerical credibility, and scientific reporting as much as in optimization.

Our central proposition is that representation freedom should be paired with evidence precision. A design process may become more adventurous in the shapes it explores while becoming more exact about the scope of every result. The conclusion of a run should therefore be an inspectable chain from candidate identity to observation to interpretation, rather than an isolated scalar or an attractive render. This is how an ambitious design program can remain scientifically legible as its complexity grows.

The remainder proceeds from representation and search to the fitness and test taxonomy, then numerical verification, retained results, agents, reproducibility, and future manufacturing applications. The online atlas supplies exact source links for all named CAD entries. The paper supplies the conceptual and technical connections needed to read that atlas as a coherent engineering system.
